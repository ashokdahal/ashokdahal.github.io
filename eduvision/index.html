My educational vision developed through movement between two learning contexts. The journey did not lead me to choose one model over another. It led me to ask a more useful question: what kind of guidance helps a particular student learn at a particular moment?

This story reflects my own educational experiences. It is not intended as a general comparison of Nepalese and Dutch education.

01 Nepal · Foundations

Structure gave me a strong starting point

My earlier education was often organised around clear explanation, disciplinary theory and systematic progression. The teacher carried substantial responsibility for selecting, explaining and sequencing knowledge. This approach helped me build conceptual foundations and develop the discipline needed to master technically demanding subjects.

What I retain Clear explanation · conceptual foundations · high expectations · systematic progression

What I later questioned: when explanation becomes the dominant mode, students can have fewer opportunities to make decisions, test ideas and learn how to act when the solution is uncertain.

02 The Netherlands · Application

Learning became something to do

My university experience in the Netherlands exposed me more strongly to discussion, independent preparation, project work and authentic problem solving. Students were expected not only to understand knowledge, but also to use it, question it and justify their choices.

What I retain Authentic application · dialogue · reflection · collaboration · student responsibility

What I also observed: autonomy is productive only when students have enough prior knowledge, confidence and support to use it. A task that is challenging for one student may be inaccessible to another.

03 My educational vision

Structure should enable autonomy

I see university teaching as the deliberate design of a progression from supported learning towards independent academic judgement. Students need clear disciplinary foundations before they can use knowledge confidently in unfamiliar situations. Direct explanation therefore has an important place in my teaching, particularly when concepts are new or technically difficult. However, explanation is not the endpoint. Its purpose is to prepare students to apply knowledge, compare alternatives, identify limitations and make increasingly independent decisions.

The amount of guidance should change with the learner and the task. Students enter my courses with different experience in programming, mathematics, geospatial analysis and machine learning. Providing identical support to everyone can therefore produce unequal opportunities to learn. I aim to diagnose what students already know, offer sufficient structure for productive participation, and then reduce that support as competence develops. Autonomy, in this view, is not the absence of teaching. It is an educational outcome that develops through well-timed scaffolding, practice and feedback.

This also changes how I understand my role as a teacher. I move between explaining, modelling, questioning, coaching and stepping back. I want students to leave a course not only knowing how a method works, but also able to decide when it is appropriate, defend their reasoning, recognise uncertainty and seek further knowledge when needed. Effective teaching therefore combines disciplinary rigour with active learning and reflection. The central criterion is not whether teaching is teacher-centred or student-centred, but whether each design decision helps students progress towards informed independence.

04 Theoretical coordinates

The ideas that orient my teaching

My vision is informed by educational theory, but I use theory as a design lens rather than as a recipe.

Professional dialogue. UTQ sessions and conversations with colleagues made the timing of guidance more explicit in my thinking. I now treat guidance as a design variable: something to increase, reduce or change in response to prior knowledge and student progress.

06 University of Twente

My vision connects with UT through responsive guidance

UT emphasises learning by doing, inclusive learning communities and self-development. These principles fit my view that students should work with authentic problems and progressively take responsibility for their own learning.

Learning by doingwork with data, code, models and uncertainty Inclusive communitiescreate different pathways into shared learning goals Self-developmentdevelop reflection, judgement and responsibility

My additional emphasis is responsive guidance. Students cannot benefit equally from active or challenge-based learning if the required entry knowledge is assumed rather than established. I therefore see scaffolding as the bridge between inclusive participation and increasing autonomy.
